01 / Where I started

“I never thought I'd be here.”

I did not take the straight path. After Biomedical Engineering at Temasek Polytechnic and National Service, my first university application was rejected.

By then I had been financially independent since 17. I ran small e-commerce and sales businesses, sourcing products from suppliers in China and arranging delivery to customers. I was doing supply chain work years before I knew what it was called.

I used my gap year to work at Ticketmaster and saw that even a ticketing business has to buy well. A few cents saved on every blank ticket adds up. That was when it clicked: I could create value by spotting the gap between what something costs and what it is worth.

I enrolled in SUSS in 2023 to study Supply Chain Management. Two years later I was awarded the Singapore-Industry Scholarship by Infineon Technologies, and my story was featured in Lianhe Zaobao.

Your past does not define you. With the right support, mindset and effort, you can shape your own future.

03 / Why semiconductors

Hidden in everything.

The chip shortage was my turning point. Cars, phones and appliances stopped shipping because of a part most people never see. When AI adoption took off in late 2023, I saw an industry set to grow fast, with a demanding pace and a truly international footprint.

Semiconductors are also one of Singapore's strategic industries. Being able to contribute to that gives me a strong sense that this is a career worth building.

Time to customer

On time, as promised.

I grew up being taught to show up when I said I would, without excuses. In supply chain that means delivering when the customer needs it. Not late, and not too early. I focus on controlling what can be controlled, so there is buffer left for what cannot. Moving on-time delivery from around 85% to above 90% is the kind of goal that drives me.

Stakeholder value

Value on both sides of the table.

At Skyworks my stakeholders were suppliers, managers and the vendors bidding for my project. I learned to state my needs clearly, understand what each supplier could really deliver, and give every bidder fair access to information. Strong ethics and relationships inside and outside the company made decisions faster.

04 / Selected work · Skyworks

An overflowing warehouse, redesigned without more space or people.

The automation project I led during my work-study year, from the first measurements to a running system.

Grant approved

What · The problem

Packed like sardines.

The warehouse was overflowing. The usual answers, more floor space or more manpower, were not options. The question was how to grow capacity and speed inside the same four walls.

What · Looking up

Part of the answer was on the ceiling.

I calculated the original capacity and modelled new layouts. ABC and material flow analysis, done as part of the project, showed which items moved most and where they should sit.

Then I noticed the ceiling lights hung low enough to limit stacking height. I asked whether they could be shortened. That one question opened up a large share of the extra space.

What · Bringing goods to people

Let the robots do the walking.

I simulated Autonomous Mobile Robots (AMRs) that bring the right pallet from the aisle to the checkout counter. Pickers no longer walk the aisles searching for material. Timed studies compared picks per hour before and after.

So what · Winning support

The hardest part was not the model.

My directors questioned whether my numbers and assumptions were safe. I worked with our vendor to explore an Economic Development Board (EDB) automation grant, and we secured the funding. That lowered the risk enough for management to approve the budget.

Throughout the tender, I made sure every bidding vendor had the same information.

So what · The outcome

+80%storage capacity in the same floor space
~250%more picks per hour, measured in timed studies
>S$1.5Mprojected savings over five years

The AMRs are now up and running.

Now what · What I took away

Numbers start a decision. Trust is what gets it approved.

Looking back, each stage of the project matched a principle I had studied in class. Applying them on a real warehouse floor showed me what they actually mean, and where the textbook version stops.

The problem

Theory of Constraints

Goldratt's idea is to find the one constraint that limits the whole system and get the most out of it before spending on more capacity. Our constraint was not floor space but height, held down by low ceiling lights.

A constraint is only fixed until someone questions it. Raising the lights cost far less than a new building.

Problem solving

Pareto principle and ABC analysis

A small share of materials made up most of the picks. Ranking them by movement told me which items deserved the closest, easiest locations.

The 80/20 rule is not just a chart in a lecture. It tells you where effort pays off first.

Developing the solution

Lean and the waste of motion

Walking and searching is one of Lean's seven wastes. Goods-to-person AMRs remove that waste instead of asking people to walk faster.

Good automation removes waste from the process, not effort from the people.

Measuring the outcome

Measure, then control

Following the Measure and Control steps of DMAIC, timed studies set a picks-per-hour baseline before the change and checked it after.

Without a baseline, an improvement is only an opinion. With one, it becomes evidence a director can trust.

Winning support

Minto's Pyramid Principle

Lead with the answer, then group the supporting arguments beneath it. I restructured my pitch so management saw the recommendation first and the evidence after.

Persuasion is not about saying more. It is about putting the right point first for the audience in the room.

Soft Skill

Communication

“Plan, craft, and deliver persuasive arguments that responsibly influence the values, attitudes, and beliefs of others.”

My first pitches were turned down, and I was told my presentation skills were weak. I took that as constructive feedback and read up on the Pyramid Principle to learn how a persuasive argument is built.

I practised on the end users first, then pitched to colleagues and managers, refining the deck after every round. The final presentation to management set out the benefits to be gained, the capital investment required, how the vendor was selected through competitive bidding and a supplier scorecard, and the data behind every number.

It reinforced my understanding of persuasive argument. A clear structure, the audience's priorities and evidence they can check. That is what turned an early rejection into an approved project.

More selected work

From supplier deals to capacity forecasts.

Sourcing · Skyworks

Reading the numbers behind a supplier.

For moisture barrier bags, which protect moisture-sensitive chips in storage and transit, I analysed our monthly usage and compared prices across suppliers. I proposed shifting most of the order volume to a new, more competitive supplier while keeping the existing supplier as a second source for supply chain resilience. The result was a 10% year-on-year saving for the next three years, with stronger supply security at the same time.

I also wrote a Python script that collects public financial data and compares indicators of each supplier's financial health. Knowing a supplier's position before a negotiation put us in a stronger spot.

Strategic sourcingDual sourcingPythonFinancial indicators

Cost and resilience do not have to be a trade-off when the sourcing strategy is designed for both.

Demand planning · Skyworks

Racing for supplier capacity.

When new products were launching, we were constantly competing for our suppliers' weekly capacity. I had to balance warehouse space, what suppliers could deliver and what we would actually use.

I built a model showing which materials needed resupply and when each resupply would become critical, so the team could act before a shortage held up the production line.

Demand planningForecastingInventory

Seeing a shortage coming is worth more than reacting fast once it arrives.

Logistics · Infineon Munich and Singapore Now

Spotting capacity problems before they happen.

While working on projects in Munich, I am also building a capacity simulation for Infineon's DCA2 warehouse in Singapore. It uses a statistical model to predict when the warehouse will run out of space, so the team can step in early.

In Munich I work on an ontology and knowledge graph for industry applications. I also trained more than 200 people in the global supply chain and logistics team on a new project management tool, then supported key users as they adopted it.

SimulationKnowledge graphsChange adoption

Sites outside headquarters do not simply follow decisions. Their open feedback helps decision makers improve them.

Capstone · SCM488 In progress

Can DCA2 handle tomorrow's volume?

My final year project asks whether Infineon's DCA2 distribution centre in Singapore can absorb future growth in product volume.

Methods. I am building a baseline of storage locations, throughput and current utilisation. I then project demand under several growth scenarios and use simulation and sensitivity analysis to test how capacity holds up as each assumption changes.

What I expect to deliver. A probability of when capacity is likely to run short, where the possible bottlenecks are, and a recommendation on when intervention may be needed.

Capacity planningScenario analysisSimulation

Testing a range of futures is more useful than betting on a single forecast.

06 / Global learning

I learn best by going to see how the world works.

I have worked with a US-headquartered semiconductor company in Singapore and a German one in Singapore and Munich, and studied in China and Germany. In each place I watched how industry leaders solve complex supply chain problems with new tools and automation.

Hangzhou2024

Alibaba Taobao Education, AI in Business

I saw how a leading e-commerce platform conduct AI trainings, then applied image generation, AI video for livestreams and text-to-music to speed up marketing. AI was still new to most people then. It showed me how a whole community can be transformed to adopt it, and sparked my interest in an AI-enabled ASEAN and China supply chain network.

Shenzhen and Guangzhou2025

NIE351 Interdisciplinary Global Learning

Visits to companies such as BYD and COSCO Shipping showed me China's scale and speed first hand. I saw how the business environment and regulation shape the way industries grow, and how different that is from the environment I know in Singapore.

Berlin2026

Humboldt University Summer School

International Commercial Litigation and Dispute Resolution (A+). International business runs on contracts, negotiation and the ability to resolve disputes across legal systems, not just on operations. In a global supply chain, understanding context matters as much as understanding process.

An Iron Kingdom? History and Legacy of the Prussian Expansion. Walking through Berlin made it an open classroom on how politics and history still shape a society today.

I cooked Bak Kut Teh and curry chicken for classmates from the UK, Spain, China and Korea. Food turned out to be the best bridge between cultures.

Munich2026

Infineon headquarters and Innovation Week

What I took on

Leadership is about impact, not titles.

Together with a 13-member Executive Committee, I ran outdoor programmes for one of SUSS' largest student communities. My role spanned finance, partnerships and events, from planning budgets to standing in the water with members on expedition.

  • Finance and data. I led financial planning and used attendance data across more than 1,000 members to justify a PassionWave corporate membership. Multiplying past sign-up rates by member discounts showed savings of over S$5,000, which won the school council's approval and secured the renewal for 2025/26. The membership was later opened to other SUSS clubs that partnered with us.
  • National record. I led the SUSS contingent at a Singapore Book of Records attempt for the largest kayak raft formation, with 438 kayaks and 577 paddlers. The Straits Times covered the event on 4 August 2025.
  • Community. I directed the 2025 YouthConnect Camp with the Children's Cancer Foundation (CCF), designing an Amazing Race that built resilience, confidence and friendship among 13 young cancer survivors. The club later received CCF's Caring Heart Silver Volunteer Award.
  • Adventure. I joined members on expeditions such as white water rafting, abseiling and caving in Gopeng, and supported our climbers at SMU Gravical 2025.
What

A student club that gives students a safe and fun place to make connections through outdoor activities and sports.

So what

I learned that empowering teammates is far more powerful than anything one person can do alone. Simon Sinek calls this the Circle of Safety (Leaders Eat Last, 2014). When people feel safe and valued, they naturally innovate and contribute far more than any top-down mandate could ever get out of them.

Now what

I bring the same mindset to my early career. Build relationships, create a space where people collaborate well, bring stakeholders along early and set up teams so others can lead too.

Community service

It starts with showing up.

I first supported the Children's Cancer Foundation by shaving my head for Hair for Hope in 2013 and 2015. Receiving their Caring Heart Silver Volunteer Award with my club years later felt like coming full circle.

Impact is never created alone. It is built through shared belief and small acts of care that add up over time.

Community service

Going back, again and again.

I run monthly sustainability workshops and organised outing to Sungei Buloh for seniors with NTUC Health, coordinate youth volunteers for Hao Ren Hao Shi, and have marshalled at the Singapore Grand Prix since 2019.

Going back has taught me that big results rarely come from one-off events. They build up from small acts of care, repeated month after month. Through the lens of Maslow's hierarchy of needs, showing up consistently helps meet the base of the pyramid. Meeting their physiological needs, safety, trust and belonging. Once those are in place, worries ease and people have room to grow, as individuals and as a community.

Seeing that change is what keeps me going and keeps burnout away. Long planning sessions and tiring schedules feel worthwhile because they connect me to something bigger than myself, which Maslow later called self-actualization. A strong volunteer network, seniors who feel more confident and connected are the goals and the reasons I keep coming back.
